Tyre: The Rise and End of the Rock in the Sea

Tyre (Hebrew צֹר Ṣōr, “rock”) — Phoenicia’s queen of the sea: from three thousand years of splendor to a reef where fishermen spread their nets. Yet on the Bible’s own storyline, the city the prophets sentenced became, in the end, a place where Jesus ministered — and where a church knelt on the beach to pray (Acts 21).

1. A City Built on a Rock in the SeaGEOGRAPHY

Tyre lies on the Mediterranean coast of southern Lebanon, about 35 kilometers south of Sidon and 45 kilometers north of Mount Carmel. Her Hebrew name צֹר (Ṣōr) simply means “rock” — not a figure of speech but a geographical fact: ancient Tyre was really two cities. One stood on the mainland shore, called “Old Tyre” (Ushu; Palaetyrus in Greek sources); the other was built on a rocky island some 600 to 800 meters offshore, ringed by the sea, its walls rising straight out of the water — reportedly up to 45 meters at their highest. This twin-city structure is the key to Tyre’s entire history, and to Ezekiel 26: the city on the mainland could be taken, but the island city in the sea defied one empire after another.

2. The Golden Age: Hiram, Purple Dye, and CarthageGOLDEN AGE

According to Herodotus, the priests of Tyre claimed their city had been founded 2,300 years before his visit — around 2750 BC. But Tyre’s true golden age came in the tenth to eighth centuries BC. King Hiram I (r. ca. 980–947 BC) allied himself with David and Solomon, supplying cedar and pine, gold and craftsmen for the palace and temple in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:11; 1 Kings 5); the Tyrian craftsman Huram cast with his own hands the temple’s two bronze pillars and the bronze Sea (1 Kings 7:13–45). That partnership wove Tyre deep into Israel’s memory — which is why the prophets’ rebukes of Tyre often carry the grief of a covenant broken by a former ally: Amos indicts her for “disregarding a treaty of brotherhood” (Amos 1:9).

Tyre’s wealth had two sources. The first was Tyrian purple: a dye extracted from the gland of the murex sea snail — thousands of snails for a single robe, so costly that the color became a byword for royalty; the phrase “royal purple” descends from it. The second was her maritime trade network: the famous lament of Ezekiel 27 pictures Tyre as a splendid merchant ship built from the finest of every nation, its cargo list — silver of Tarshish, bronze of Javan, lambs of Arabia, spices of Sheba — reading almost like a master catalogue of ancient Near Eastern world trade. Tyre also planted colonies, most famously Carthage, founded in 814 BC by the fugitive princess Elissa (Dido) — the “New City” (Qart-ḥadašt) that would one day contest the Mediterranean with Rome. One more thread: Jezebel was the daughter of Ethbaal, king of Tyre and Sidon (1 Kings 16:31); through that marriage Baal worship moved north and became the backdrop of the spiritual battle of Elijah’s day.

3. The Shadow of Empires: Assyria and BabylonASSYRIA & BABYLON

From the ninth century BC onward, Tyre paid tribute to Assyria. Shalmaneser V besieged the city for five years (ca. 725–720 BC); Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal in turn marched against her — mainland Old Tyre changed hands again and again, but the island city never fell. The truly long trial came from Babylon: not long after Jerusalem’s fall, Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for thirteen years (ca. 586/585–573 BC, according to Josephus citing the Phoenician historian Menander). In the end Tyre accepted Babylonian overlordship; members of the royal house were deported, and Babylonian administrative tablets record a king of Tyre drawing royal rations. Yet the island city was not sacked — its wealth had long since moved by sea.

Here is a detail no student of the prophets can walk past. Ezekiel 29:17–20 — the latest-dated oracle in the whole book, 571 BC — states plainly that Nebuchadnezzar and his army got “no reward” from Tyre, and that the Lord therefore gives him Egypt as wages. The text itself concedes that thirteen years of siege did not yield the expected plunder — and that unvarnished honesty is strong evidence for the book’s credibility: had the prophecy been invented after the fact, an editor could easily have smoothed the tension away. It also signals that the image of chapter 26 — “many nations … like the sea casting up its waves” (26:3) — was never about a single campaign: Nebuchadnezzar was only the first wave.

“Therefore this is what the Sovereign Lord says: I am against you, Tyre, and I will bring many nations against you, like the sea casting up its waves. They will destroy the walls of Tyre and pull down her towers; I will scrape away her rubble and make her a bare rock. Out in the sea she will become a place to spread fishnets …” — Ezekiel 26:3–5 (an oracle of 587/586 BC)

4. 332 BC: Alexander Fills In the SeaALEXANDER, 332 BC

The second and fiercest wave surged in about 254 years after Ezekiel prophesied. In 332 BC, Alexander, marching east, asked to enter the island city to sacrifice to Melqart (the Baal of Tyre). Tyre politely declined — the islanders still believed their city impregnable. Alexander’s answer rewrote the geography: having no navy, he ordered the debris of mainland Old Tyre — stones, timber, rubble — dumped into the sea to build a causeway to the island (a mole), roughly 60 meters wide and nearly a kilometer long. The classical historians Arrian and Curtius both record the project; anyone who has read Ezekiel 26:12 — “they will … throw your stones, timber and rubble into the sea” — finds it hard not to pause here. Ezekiel 26:8–11 describes Nebuchadnezzar’s assault with a singular “he”; from 26:12 the text shifts to a plural “they” — and the literal act of “throwing into the sea” was carried out by the second wave of attackers.

The siege lasted seven months. When the Tyrians burned the first causeway with fire-ships, Alexander gathered 223 warships from already-subdued Sidon, Byblos, and Arados, and from Cyprus, blockaded both harbors, and raised at the head of the mole two siege towers about 50 meters tall, sheathed in raw hides against fire — by report the tallest in ancient military history. In July 332 BC the southern wall was breached, and the Macedonians poured in through the gap and from the sea at once. Six to eight thousand defenders fell; two thousand were crucified on the beach; some thirty thousand inhabitants were sold into slavery. Only King Azemilkos and the few who fled into the temple of Melqart were spared. The rock-city in the sea had been taken for the first time.

5. The Gospel Comes to Tyre: the Footsteps of Jesus and a Base of PrayerTHE GOSPEL COMES TO TYRE

Remarkably, Tyre did not vanish from the map in 332 BC. Alexander resettled the city; it revived in the Hellenistic era, and under Rome became the capital of the province of Phoenicia with the rank of a colonia. Most of the grandest ruins in today’s Tyre belong to that period: a hippodrome about 480 meters long seating tens of thousands (among the largest of antiquity), a monumental arch, colonnaded avenues, and a necropolis — inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 1984. And it was in this Romanized Tyre that the biblical narrative turned a wholly new page.

Jesus Himself Steps into the Land under Sentence

The Gospels give Tyre far more weight than one might expect. First the Tyrians come to Jesus: Mark records that a great crowd came to him from “around Tyre and Sidon” “when they heard about all he was doing” (Mark 3:8; cf. Luke 6:17) — the Gentile sea-city under sentence of judgment appears on the roster of seekers. Then Jesus himself goes to them: leaving Galilee, he “went to the vicinity of Tyre” and Sidon (Mark 7:24; Matthew 15:21) — the northernmost journey the Gospels record, onto the very soil Ezekiel had sentenced. There the Syrophoenician (Canaanite) woman pleads for her daughter, and Jesus commends her: “Woman, you have great faith!” (Matthew 15:28). A Gentile’s great faith is sealed by the Lord’s own lips within the borders of Tyre — a first sign of the gospel crossing Israel’s boundary. And Jesus said that if the miracles done in Chorazin and Bethsaida “had been performed in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes,” so that “it will be more bearable for Tyre and Sidon on the day of judgment” (Matthew 11:21–22; Luke 10:13–14) — in the Lord’s mouth, the judged city becomes the yardstick against which the unbelief of the chosen people is measured.

Acts 21: a Base of Prayer

By the book of Acts, Tyre is no longer merely a mission field but a rooted community of faith. At the end of Paul’s third journey his ship unloaded at Tyre, and “we sought out the disciples there and stayed with them seven days” (Acts 21:4) — “sought out the disciples” shows a church already long established, likely fruit of those scattered after Stephen’s death who “traveled as far as Phoenicia,” preaching the word (Acts 11:19). Moved by the Spirit, these disciples urged Paul not to go up to Jerusalem; the farewell is one of the most moving scenes in Acts: “All of them, including wives and children, accompanied us out of the city, and there on the beach we knelt to pray. After saying goodbye to each other, we went aboard the ship” (Acts 21:5–6). The believers of a whole city, with their wives and children, kneeling together on the sand — very possibly beside the sandbar silted up along Alexander’s causeway. The shore the prophet had marked out as “a place to spread fishnets” had become a base of prayer; the nets on the rocks and the kneeling church overlap on the same coastline. And the line runs on: the church father Origen was buried at Tyre; around AD 315 Eusebius of Caesarea delivered, at the dedication of Tyre’s great basilica, one of the most celebrated addresses of early church history.

6. The End: the Long Silence after 1291THE END

The Crusaders took Tyre in 1124, and once more she mattered greatly: coronation church of the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem, seat of the chronicler William of Tyre as archbishop; after Frederick Barbarossa drowned on the Third Crusade, his bones were reportedly laid in Tyre’s cathedral. But after Acre fell in May 1291, the garrison abandoned Tyre without a fight, and the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Khalil ordered all fortifications demolished so the Crusaders could never return. The purple-dye industry that had sustained Tyre for over two millennia ended with it.

This time the city truly died, and stayed dead a long while. Passing through in 1355, Ibn Battuta wrote of “a mass of ruins”; when the English scholar Henry Maundrell visited in 1697, he found only “a few poor families, subsisting upon fishing.” Travelers saw the fishermen’s nets spread on the rocks — and Ezekiel 26:5, 14, “a place to spread fishnets,” became one of the scenes most often cited in the literature of apologetics. Only around 1750 did the local sheikh Nassif al-Nassar begin rebuilding; by the late nineteenth century the town numbered about five thousand. Today’s Tyre (Sour) is Lebanon’s fifth-largest city, some sixty thousand people in town — a fishing port and heritage city of the south, and repeatedly struck in the recent wars (2006; 2023–24).

7. Ezekiel 26 and the Echo of HistoryPROPHECY & HISTORY

The Declaration of Ezekiel 26The Echo of History
“I will bring many nations against you, like the sea casting up its waves” (26:3)Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome, the Crusaders, the Mamluks — the attackers did come wave upon wave, across nineteen centuries.
Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon will batter “your walls” with rams (26:7–11; singular “he”)The thirteen-year siege of 586–573 BC; the mainland city destroyed, the island reduced to vassalage. 29:17–20 concedes he took no plunder.
“they will … throw your stones, timber and rubble into the sea” (26:12; now plural “they”)In 332 BC Alexander poured the debris of Old Tyre into the sea to build his causeway — “into the sea” in the most literal sense.
“I will scrape away her rubble and make her a bare rock … a place to spread fishnets” (26:4–5, 14)For centuries after 1291, travelers describe fishermen’s nets among the ruined rocks (Maundrell, 1697).
“You will never be rebuilt, for I the Lord have spoken” (26:14)See the discussion below.

On 26:14 — “you will never be rebuilt” — honest study must face a fact: today’s Tyre is a living city. Scholarship and the interpretive tradition offer three main approaches. First, the word concerns the end of Tyre the island fortress and royal city — that city-state, that political and commercial entity, never returned after 332 (at the latest, 1291); there is no continuity between modern Sour and the old queen of the sea. Second, the prophecy’s focus is mainland Old Tyre, leveled for causeway material — and it was never rebuilt. Third, chapter 26 uses the prophets’ type-language of “utter overthrow” (compare the similar wording of the oracles against Babylon and Edom), its fulfillment unfolding progressively in history and pointing to eschatological completion. Each reading has textual footing, and all three remind us that a prophetic oracle is not meant to supply a checkable construction schedule; it proclaims the Lord’s sovereignty over the proud city — precisely the theme of the oracle against the king of Tyre in chapter 28: “Your heart became proud on account of your beauty” (28:17). Isaiah 23 likewise lifts a burden over Tyre, yet ends with a surprising turn: after seventy years her profit “will be set apart for the Lord” (Isaiah 23:18). At the far end of judgment stands the prospect of the nations coming to the Lord — and Psalm 87:4 counts Tyre among those who acknowledge him.

Conclusion: Scripture as the Main Line, History’s Ending as the Side LineTHE BIBLICAL VANTAGE POINT

Having walked the whole route, we must put the order right: the historical evidence of sections 6 and 7 — the causeway, the ruins, the fishermen with their nets — is valuable corroboration, but it is the side line; the Bible’s own narrative of Tyre is the main line. That line forms a complete arc of redemptive history: Hiram works with Solomon on the temple (Kings); the prophets announce judgment on the proud city (Isaiah 23; Ezekiel 26–28; Amos 1:9–10; Zechariah 9:2–4); Jesus himself walks into the sentenced land to preach, to heal, and to seal a Gentile woman’s great faith (Mark 7; Matthew 15); and by Acts 21 Tyre has a church that kneels on the beach, wives and children and all, to pray. Psalm 87:4 had long foretold that Tyre would be counted among those who acknowledge the Lord, and Isaiah 23:18 that her earnings would in the end be “set apart for the Lord” — words fulfilled not in the Roman hippodrome but in the disciples kneeling on the shore.

In other words, to read Ezekiel 26 through Alexander’s causeway alone is to read the prophet too small. History’s ending tells us that not one word of God’s judgment on pride falls to the ground; Scripture’s main line tells us more — that judgment is never God’s last word to the nations. In the canon, Tyre’s “end” is not the bare rock but the church praying upon the rock: what was torn down was the pride of the queen of the sea; what is being built up is the body of Christ. And that is the interpretive rule of this whole series: every piece of historical and archaeological material is finally set back into the whole canon and the course of redemptive history, and weighed there.
